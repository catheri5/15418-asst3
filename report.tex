\section{Shared Baseline and Optimizations}
\label{sec:shared}

\subsection{Exhaustive route generation and cost calculation}

We generate routes from an index instead of storing every possible route in a
vector. The index tells \texttt{route\_from\_index()} where to place the bends.
We construct one route, score it, and discard it unless it is the best so far.
This reduces allocation and memory traffic while still checking every legal
route.

\subsection{Shared locality and construction optimizations}

Both modes use the following constant-factor optimizations:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Indexed route generation.} We generate one route from its index,
score it, and then move to the next index. This avoids allocating and storing
every candidate route at once.
\item \textbf{Horizontal and vertical scoring loops.} We use separate loops for
horizontal and vertical segments instead of checking a segment's direction for
every cell. This is a small optimization that saves on some checks in the inner most loop.
\item \textbf{Transposed and Normal Occupancy.} \texttt{occupancy[y][x]} makes
and \texttt{occupancy\_columns[x][y]} make horizontal and vertical pieces contiguous in memory.
This reduces cache misses since you can load entire lines in the direction that you are traversing.
\end{itemize}

\section{Within-Wires: Design and Performance Debugging Journey}
\label{sec:within}

\subsection{Implemented optimizations and final parallelization}

W mode keeps annealing iterations and the outer wire loop sequential. For one
wire, it removes the old route, evaluates legal candidates in parallel, reduces
the workers' best routes, and commits one winning route. The implemented
optimizations are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Potential wire position per thread} One complete candidate route is the
unit of parallel work; candidates for the same removed wire are independent.
\item \textbf{No candidate vector.} At first, the code generated every route into a vector
and parallelized only scoring. The final code constructs, scores, and discards
one candidate at a time, eliminating large temporary storage.
\item \textbf{Indexed direct construction.} A route index determines its bend
pattern. Workers therefore construct and score their own candidates, rather
than waiting for one thread to build all routes.
\item \textbf{Exact route counts and random-route construction.}
\texttt{count\_routes()} determines loop bounds and the fallback decision
without materializing routes; the random branch constructs only its selected
route.
\item \textbf{Private bests and reusable buffers.} Each worker tracks a private
best cost and route, then writes one result into storage allocated once outside
the wire loop. This means we only have to synchronize at the end.
\item \textbf{Thresholded static scheduling.} Searches with fewer than four
candidates per requested thread run sequentially. Larger searches use
\texttt{schedule(static,1)}, assigning complete candidate routes cyclically.
\item \textbf{Direction-specific scoring and two occupancy views.} The shared
horizontal/vertical loops and row-major/transposed occupancy arrays make scans
contiguous in the direction being traversed.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Synchronization and correctness}

During one candidate search, workers only read occupancy; the current wire was
removed before the search, so each candidate cell uses
$(occupancy[y][x]+1)^2$. Workers write only their own private best route and
cost. The worksharing loop has one barrier, followed by one reduction. Removing
and inserting occupancy is sequential between wires. This avoids per-candidate
locks, atomics, and critical sections.

\subsection{Optimization Decision Making and Tradeoffs}

We tested scheduling and granularity on medium input at eight threads. Static
scheduling measured 1.5045~s versus 1.5355~s for dynamic scheduling. With
static scheduling, chunk one measured 1.4862~s versus 1.5134~s for chunk four;
the differences were small, but favored inexpensive candidate-level static
scheduling. We therefore retained static, chunk-one scheduling and the
small-search fallback rather than adding a dynamic task queue.

The verified final GHC results in Table 7 and
Figure 2 show W eight-thread computation speedups of
$7.70\times$, $7.20\times$, and $5.95\times$ on few, medium, and abundant
inputs. The shared indexed construction and dual occupancy views reduce
allocation traffic and improve horizontal and vertical scan locality.

\subsection{Limitations against Perfect Speedup}

W exposes candidates for only one wire at a time. Some wires have too few
candidates to keep all 128 workers busy, and every wire requires a barrier,
reduction, and sequential occupancy update before the next search starts, 
causing large synchronization overhead if the space of possible wires is small 
compared to amount of wires.
Data movement is a contributing cost, but it is not by itself a complete
explanation for the W-mode limit.  On GHC, total W cache misses do rise as
threads are added: the medium case rises from 27.48 million at one thread to
33.35 million at eight, and the abundant case from 114.05 million to 135.90
million.  This is evidence of additional cache and memory pressure.  However,
even with ideal cache behavior, W would still expose only one wire's candidates
at a time and would still pay one barrier, reduction, and serial occupancy
update per wire.  Those structural costs set a concurrency limit independently
of data movement; data movement amplifies it rather than replacing it as an
explanation.

We also considered splitting one candidate route's cell score across several
workers, or parallelizing the final reduction of per-worker best routes. We
rejected both as too fine grained. The existing design already distributes
whole candidate routes across up to 128 workers. A second level of parallelism
would require reductions and extra barriers for every candidate, while the
final reduction itself has at most 127 comparisons at 128 threads. That
synchronization overhead is much larger than the small amount of work it could
remove.

On GHC, W improves monotonically through eight threads; there is no measured
drop-off in that range. On PSC, however, W eventually plateaus or regresses at
high thread counts. We attribute that behavior to the combination of the
one-wire-at-a-time work limit and repeated synchronization, with the observed
cache/memory pressure making the plateau worse once each wire has too little
candidate work to amortize the overhead.

\section{Across-Wires: Design and Performance Debugging Journey}
\label{sec:across}

\subsection{Implemented optimizations and final parallelization}

A mode uses persistent threads instead of restarting them for every annealing
iteration. In an iteration, a worker repeatedly claims one batch of wires. It
first selects a replacement route for every wire in that batch, without changing
occupancy. It then commits all changed wires in that batch by atomically
removing each old route and atomically inserting its replacement. Different
batches can be selected and committed concurrently. The worksharing barrier at
the end of the iteration ensures every batch has committed before selection for
the next iteration begins. Unlike W mode, a worker leaves a wire's old route in
occupancy while selecting its replacement, because another worker may be
scoring that same occupancy cell. The implemented optimizations are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Batch of wires per worker.} A whole batch is the unit of
parallel work. Dynamic scheduling assigns the next unclaimed batch to the next
available worker, which helps when wires have different candidate counts.
\item \textbf{Persistent threads.} Threads are created once rather than once
per annealing iteration. This avoids repeatedly creating and destroying OpenMP
workers.
\item \textbf{Private random state and reusable route updates.} Each worker
has its own random-number generator and remembers its chosen routes in a
reusable private buffer. The random generator performs annealing's
probabilistic candidate choice; keeping it private avoids a shared-state lock
and repeated allocation while scoring a batch.
\item \textbf{Atomic occupancy accesses.} Workers atomically read both
occupancy views while scoring and atomically decrement/increment them when
committing routes. These operations prevent C++ data races while allowing
separate batches to make progress concurrently.
\item \textbf{Overlap intervals instead of per-cell route membership tests.}
The first A scorer called \texttt{cell\_on\_wire()} at every candidate cell to
find whether it overlapped the old route. The final scorer computes the small
set of segment-overlap ranges once, then scores contiguous overlapping and
non-overlapping spans directly.
\item \textbf{Correct overlap scoring.} A candidate cell shared with the old
route uses $value^2$, since removing the old route and adding the candidate
cancel. All other cells use $(value+1)^2$. The interval representation applies
this required A-mode rule without changing the cost function.
\item \textbf{Direction-specific scoring and two occupancy views.} The shared
horizontal/vertical loops use the row-major and transposed matrices so that
each segment is traversed contiguously in its own direction.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Synchronization and correctness}

The OpenMP worksharing loop has an implicit barrier at the end of each
iteration. That is the synchronization point separating one iteration's batch
commits from the next iteration's route selection; no global lock or critical
section is used around the complete scoring loop. While a worker scores every
candidate cell, it uses an atomic read from the appropriate occupancy view,
because another worker may be committing a different batch at the same time.
After choosing every wire in its batch, the worker commits each change with
atomic updates: it decrements every cell of the old route and increments every
cell of the replacement route in both the row-major and transposed occupancy
views. The atomics prevent data races, but they do not make the many reads of
one candidate route into one globally consistent snapshot.

We determine overlaps between each candidate segment and the old wire before
visiting that segment's cells. The scorer walks non-overlapping spans using $(value+1)^2$ and overlapping
spans using $value^2$, where removing the old wire and adding the candidate
cancel. This replaces a segment scan at every cell with a small fixed-size
range calculation per segment. 
\subsection{Optimization Decision Making and Tradeoffs}

An early one-thread A-mode run was much slower than W mode on the same input.
Since that was a one-thread observation, it pointed to per-candidate scoring
work rather than lock contention or a poor distribution of batches. We used
that observation to prioritize the following decisions.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Overlap intervals.} The original scorer checked whether every
candidate cell lay on the old route. We replaced that repeated geometric work
with a constant-size overlap calculation per candidate segment. The
scoring-only diagnostic below was the direct evidence for this change.
\item \textbf{Dynamic, one-wire batches.} Wires whose endpoints are farther
apart have more candidate routes to consider, so a dynamic work queue avoids
leaving a worker with a long wire while others are idle. We retained batch size one because larger
batches defer each wire's update and did not provide a robust performance
benefit in our experiments. A guided-scheduling trial was also within normal
run-to-run variation, so we kept the simpler dynamic policy.
\item \textbf{Persistent workers, private RNGs, and reusable update buffers.}
These choices remove repeated thread startup, shared random-state locking, and
per-batch allocations. They are low-risk overhead reductions that do not alter
the candidate set or the A-mode update semantics.
\item \textbf{Atomic accesses remain enabled.} Atomics are required while
independent batches can score and commit concurrently. We tried a special
single-thread path without them, but the measured cycle improvement was only
about 1.5\%; we reverted that extra code rather than complicating the final
implementation.
\item \textbf{Two directional occupancy views.} The scorer dominates A-mode
runtime, so keeping horizontal and vertical scans contiguous is worthwhile even
though each commit must update both views.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations against Perfect Speedup}

A mode has more available parallel work than W mode, but it still cannot achieve
perfect $P\times$ speedup. Wires whose endpoints are farther apart have more
candidate routes to consider, which creates unequal workloads. Dynamic scheduling reduces that
imbalance, but each one-wire batch still incurs scheduling overhead and the last
few long wires can leave workers idle. At sufficiently high thread counts there
are also simply too few independent wire batches: the few-input case has only
128 wires, so its PSC A-mode curve levels off around 32 threads.

The other limit is shared occupancy traffic. Every candidate-cell score performs
an atomic read, and every accepted route performs atomic decrements and
increments in both occupancy views. When concurrent routes touch nearby cells,
these operations create cache-coherence and memory-bandwidth traffic; the
transposed view improves scan locality but cannot remove that sharing. The final
GHC eight-thread A-mode speedups of $7.35\times$, $6.88\times$, and
$6.40\times$ for few, medium, and abundant inputs reflect this combination of
remaining imbalance, dynamic-scheduling overhead, and atomic/data-movement
costs. PSC shows that medium and abundant inputs have enough wire-level work to
scale much farther, but total speedup still falls below computation speedup once
serial initialization becomes noticeable.

\subsection{Discussion of speedup results}

At eight threads, both modes scale well for every input: W obtains computation
speedups of $7.81\times$, $7.30\times$, and $6.43\times$ for few, medium, and
abundant inputs, while A obtains $8.45\times$, $8.56\times$, and
$8.47\times$. One possible explanation for the super linear speed up is much better 
cache hits compared to the single thread version.

The two strategies diverge at high core counts. W peaks at 64 threads on few
and medium, and at 32 threads on abundant, before flattening or regressing.
For medium and abundant inputs, candidate searches for individual wires cannot
always supply enough balanced work to amortize repeated parallel-region and
barrier costs. 

A-mode instead reaches $96.36\times$ computation speedup on medium and
$107.60\times$ on abundant at 128 threads. These results are consistent with
A-mode's larger pool of concurrently selectable wires. Few-input A-mode levels
off around 32 threads because only 128 wires are available and the fixed
initialization cost becomes a larger fraction of total time. However, medium
A-mode falls from $96.36\times$ computation speedup to $86.72\times$ total
speedup at 128 threads because initialization remains largely serial and becomes
a substantial fraction of the increasingly short end-to-end runtime.

Table~\ref{tab:reference-one-thread} compares our PSC one-thread computation
medians with the handout's good-performance reference. W-mode is close to the
reference on few and medium and is slower on abundant. A-mode's one-thread
time is higher than the reference, even though its high-core-count scaling is

$5.95\times$ for few, medium, and abundant inputs, compared with PSC values of
$7.81\times$, $7.30\times$, and $6.43\times$. Within-wires exposes candidates of only one wire at a

$6.40\times$, whereas PSC reports $8.45\times$, $8.56\times$, and

instead of exposing only one wire's candidate routes at a time, A mode gives
workers a shared pool of selectable wires. The medium and abundant inputs have
enough independent wire work to continue scaling through 128 threads, unlike W
mode, which plateaus earlier. The exception is the few test, with only 128 wires, it levels off around 32 threads as there aren't enough wires to saturate the rest of the threads

threads increase: on medium input they change from 28.87 million at one thread

occupancy cells to create more contention and possible false sharing, which intuitively would increase the number of cache misses. 
In contrast, W-mode aggregate misses rise on
the medium and abundant inputs, consistent with its repeated rereading of the
shared occupancy arrays during separate per-wire searches.

\section{Sensitivity Studies on the GHC Machines}

\subsection{Sensitivity to the probability of choosing a random route}

The required A-mode probability sweep on medium input was stable: computation
speedup at eight threads was $7.39\times$ for $P=0.01$, $7.40\times$ for
$P=0.1$, and $7.39\times$ for $P=0.5$. Higher $P$ reduces absolute work
(median one-thread computation times 10.697, 9.750, and 5.504 seconds), but
does not materially change parallel efficiency. A larger $P$ replaces more
exhaustive candidate searches with one random-route choice. This probability is
sampled independently for every wire decision by the worker that owns that
wire, so all runs both replace roughly the same fraction of
their expensive searches. The work decreases at both thread counts, leaving
their speedup ratio nearly unchanged.

\subsection{Sensitivity to problem size}

For the problem-size studies, eight-thread A-mode speedups were $7.20\times$,
$6.39\times$, and $7.36\times$ for grid dimensions 512, 2048, and 8192.
With 256, 1024, and 4096 wires on the 2048 grid, they were $7.39\times$,
$7.27\times$, and $6.38\times$, respectively. Thus A mode generally has
enough independent wire work to scale well at eight workers; the largest
wire-count case shows the expected loss from greater occupancy interference.
The grid-size trend is not monotonic because larger grids simultaneously change
the candidate work per wire and the occupancy working set. In contrast, adding
more wires directly creates more concurrent atomic accesses to the same
occupancy matrix, making the decline at 4096 wires consistent with increased
contention.
